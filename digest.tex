\section{Introduction}

 DC distribution is emerging as a key enabler for the next generation of AI/HPC data centers. Power densities are moving beyond 100~kW per rack toward megawatt-scale compute blocks, motivating architectures at 400~V and 800~V with multiple sources (utility converters, battery storage, and local capacitances) to manage fast load transients and provide redundancy. In these systems, fault clearing must be fast enough to avoid widespread voltage collapse and downtime, while remaining selective to isolate only the faulted section.

 Unlike AC networks, DC faults exhibit a rapid, non-oscillatory current rise and have no natural current zero crossing, complicating interruption and coordination~\cite{ravyts_fuse,cairoli_sscb_review}. Moreover, each source class contributes with distinct transient characteristics (e.g., capacitor discharge, battery internal impedance response, converter current limiting and fault-blocking behavior) that strongly shape both the peak current and the post-fault voltage trajectory~\cite{converter_fault_blocking}. These dynamics are central for assessing equipment withstand (peak current and $I^2t$) and for verifying that protection operates quickly enough to preserve service continuity.

 Existing standard practice does not yet provide a scalable calculation method for such multi-source, converter-dominated DC microgrids. IEC~61660 offers guidance for auxiliary DC installations and selected source types, but does not cover modern DC/DC converter behavior or time-varying bus voltages following a fault~\cite{iec61660,nvidia_800vdc}. As a result, designers cannot reliably demonstrate by calculation that:
 (i) protection devices can interrupt the prospective fault current,
 (ii) the cleared-fault voltage stays above ride-through limits, and
 (iii) the protection system remains selective.

 This digest proposes a practical, simulation-based methodology for DC short-circuit protection evaluation that can be applied with standard engineering inputs, without requiring differential-equation expertise. The methodology computes transient fault metrics (peak current, rise time, $I^2t$, and voltage recovery) and maps these to device selection and coordination checks, enabling inspection bodies and system designers to assess protection adequacy in complex DC architectures.

\section{Methodology}

 The workflow in Fig.~\ref{fig:methodology} structures protection evaluation around engineering questions that map directly to device requirements. Starting from a system description (topology, source characteristics, and cable data), a set of credible fault locations and fault types is generated. Time-domain simulation is used to compute prospective fault current and voltage trajectories for each scenario. From these waveforms, the method extracts peak current, rise time, and $I^2t$ to verify interruption capability, as well as voltage drop and recovery metrics to assess load ride-through. A coordination step then checks selectivity across protection zones by comparing device operating times and energy let-through margins.

\subsection{Key Findings}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Prospective fault current and $I^2t$ differ significantly between locations due to the mix of capacitor discharge, battery contribution, and converter current limiting/fault blocking.
    \item Voltage collapse and recovery metrics provide a direct link between protection operating time and service continuity, complementing peak-current checks.
    \item A zone-based coordination check can be evaluated systematically across scenarios to verify selectivity and backup protection assumptions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}

 The proposed workflow supports repeatable protection studies for converter-dominated DC architectures and provides a basis for defining standardized reporting (fault metrics, coordination margins, and voltage ride-through criteria). Solid-state interruption technologies can reduce energy let-through and improve selectivity, but require consistent, system-level evaluation methods to translate component ratings into deployment-ready protection schemes.

\section{Conclusion}

 This digest motivates the need for scalable DC short-circuit protection evaluation in emerging 400~V and 800~V data center microgrids and outlines a simulation-based workflow to compute protection-relevant transient metrics and perform coordination checks. Over the next 2--5 years, the key step for broader adoption will be convergence toward shared engineering practice: standardized converter fault models, comparable reporting of peak/$I^2t$/voltage metrics, and test protocols that connect system simulations to protection device performance. Future work will extend the workflow toward automated scenario coverage, parameter sensitivity for uncertain source behavior, and integration with digital design environments to accelerate protection studies during architecture exploration.
