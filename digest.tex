\begin{abstract}
As AI and high-performance computing (HPC) drive data center power densities toward the megawatt-per-row scale, 800~V DC distribution architectures are the key to enabling IT racks with power densities above 400 kW. While the industry demand for these systems is clear, a practical protection design remains a challenge for the majority of system integrators. This digest addresses this gap by presenting one of the first 'how-to' workflows for DC short-circuit protection design in multi-source, converter-dominated environments. The proposed simulation-based methodology aims to maximize compute availability by focusing on the three critical requirements for selective and efficient system protection: ensuring interruption capability through peak current analysis, maintaining speed of operation to prevent voltage collapse, and verifying selective coordination. The full paper will provide an in-depth exploration of this framework, including application to an 800~V reference design and the disclosure of extensive laboratory validation results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

 DC power distribution emerged as a key enabler for the next generation of AI data centers. Power densities are moving towards megawatt-scale compute blocks, that require 800~V DC feeds with redundant sources (utility grid, battery storage, renewable energy sources, etc.) to manage both fast load transients, idle periods and guarantee the highest compute availability~\cite{nvidia_800vdc}. In these systems, selective fault clearing is critical. It must be fast enough to minimize blast radius and avoid LVDC system voltage collapse, while maintaining selectivity.

 Unlike AC networks, DC faults exhibit a rapid, non-oscillatory current rise and have no natural current zero crossing, complicating both interruption and protection device coordination~\cite{ravyts_fuse,cairoli_sscb_review}. Moreover, each DC source type contributes with distinct transient characteristics (e.g., capacitor discharge, battery internal impedance response, converter current limiting and fault-blocking behavior) that define both the peak current and the post-fault voltage trajectory. These dynamics are central for assessing equipment withstand capabilities (peak current and $I^2t$) and for verifying that protection operates quickly enough to preserve compute continuity.

 Existing industry standard practice does not yet provide a scalable calculation method for such multi-source, converter-dominated DC microgrids. IEC~61660 offers guidance for auxiliary DC installations and selected source types, but does not cover (isolated) DC/DC converter behavior or time-varying bus voltages following a fault~\cite{iec61660}. As a result, designers cannot reliably demonstrate by calculation that:
 (i) protection devices can interrupt the prospective fault current,
 (ii) the cleared-fault voltage stays above ride-through limits, and
 (iii) the protection device closest to the faulted section can interrupt the fault current first (selectivity).

 This paper proposes a practical, simulation-based methodology for evaluating and designing DC short-circuit protection. The approach integrates component-level transient models into a unified framework that can be applied using standard engineering specifications, removing the need for specialized differential-equation expertise. By computing critical transient metrics---such as peak current, rise time, $I^2t$, and voltage recovery---the methodology enables designers and inspection bodies to rigorously assess protection adequacy and selectivity in complex, multi-source DC architectures.

\section{Methodology}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \input{figures/methodology_flowchart}
    \caption{Protection evaluation workflow: from system inputs through transient simulation to a protection report with device ratings and coordination margins.}
    \label{fig:methodology}

The methodology (Fig.~\ref{fig:methodology}) structures protection evaluation around the engineering questions that map directly to device requirements. It extends the principles of IEC~61660~\cite{iec61660} to converter-dominated topologies by replacing the standard's per-source analytical envelopes with a unified time-domain simulation that captures multi-source interactions, converter behavior, and bus voltage dynamics.

\subsection{Component Modeling}
Each source is represented by a transient-capable model parameterized from standard engineering specifications (datasheets and cable tables). Batteries are modeled as a voltage source behind an internal impedance; AC/DC rectifiers contribute a capacitor-discharge transient followed by a current-limited steady-state feed; DC/DC converters are classified as either \emph{fault-blocking} (ceasing current injection upon detecting a downstream fault) or \emph{non-fault-blocking} (continuing to feed fault current). This classification is critical: a non-isolated DC/DC converter connecting a battery bank may allow the battery to contribute additional short-circuit current through the converter's freewheeling path. Cables are modeled as series $RL$ impedances.

\subsection{Fault Scenario Generation}
For a given topology, the method generates a set of credible bolted-fault locations at each bus and feeder. At each location, the prospective fault current and bus voltage trajectories are computed via time-domain simulation.

\subsection{Protection-Relevant Metrics}
From each simulated waveform the method extracts: (1)~peak prospective current $I_\mathrm{peak}$ and time-to-peak, (2)~energy let-through $I^2t$ over a defined clearing window, and (3)~the voltage trajectory $V(t)$ at adjacent buses. These metrics feed three protection checks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Interruption capability} --- $I_\mathrm{peak} \leq I_\mathrm{cu}$: the prospective peak current must not exceed the rated breaking capacity of the protection device.
    \item \textbf{Speed of operation} --- the device must clear the fault before the bus voltage drops below the ride-through threshold (e.g.\ $0.8 \times V_\mathrm{nom}$), preserving compute continuity on healthy feeders.
    \item \textbf{Selective coordination} --- the device closest to the fault must trip first; upstream devices act as backup only if the downstream device fails to clear.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Case Study: 800~V DC Datacenter}

The methodology is applied to an 800~V DC subsystem representative of next-generation AI data center architectures (Fig.~\ref{fig:system_architecture}). The topology follows a \emph{4-to-make-3} redundancy scheme: four 3~MW AC/DC rectifiers feed a common 800~V DC bus, where any three can sustain the full load if one fails. Each 1~MW server rack row is supplied from a distribution panel via a 20~m feeder. At each distribution panel, two independent fault-current sources are present: the AC/DC infeed and a battery storage unit connected through a DC/DC converter. Branch feeders of 20~m connect the distribution panels to individual rack rows.

A bolted short-circuit fault is applied at the close-in terminals of protection device Q-12 (start of the feeder to a rack row). Fig.~\ref{fig:simulation_results} shows the resulting transients.

\textbf{Interruption capability.} The prospective peak current of 17.9~kA (Fig.~\ref{fig:fault_start_current}) defines the minimum required breaking capacity $I_\mathrm{cu}$ for Q-12. This value results from the superposition of the AC/DC rectifier capacitor discharge and the battery's contribution through the DC/DC converter. A conventional molded-case circuit breaker rated at 10~kA would be inadequate; a solid-state circuit breaker (SSCB) or a high-performance fuse with sufficient $I_\mathrm{cu}$ is required.

\textbf{Speed of operation.} The bus voltage drops below the 80\% ride-through threshold within 0.7~ms of fault inception (Fig.~\ref{fig:fault_start_voltage}). This leaves an extremely narrow window for fault detection and interruption before adjacent, healthy rack rows experience a voltage sag that may trigger compute errors. Protection devices with sub-millisecond detection and clearing times---characteristic of SSCBs---are therefore essential at this protection level.

\textbf{Selective coordination.} Q-12 must clear the fault before any upstream device (e.g.\ the main bus-tie breaker) trips. The simulation provides the time-current trajectory needed to verify that Q-12's operating characteristic lies entirely below the upstream device's curve for the computed fault current range, ensuring that only the faulted feeder is disconnected.

\section{Conclusion}

The widespread industry interest in high-power DC distribution for data centers necessitates a practical 'how-to' for designing robust short-circuit protection This paper addresses this gap by presenting the first-of-a-kind simulation-based workflows specifically tailored for the complexities of converter-dominated, multi-source DC architectures. By focusing on the critical pillars of protection design---verifying peak current for interruption capability, ensuring speed of operation to prevent voltage collapse, and demonstrating selective coordination---the methodology provides a scalable framework for next-generation 800~V systems. 

This digest establishes the core principles; the full paper will explain the modeling details, applying the methodology to an 800~V reference design and disclosing extensive laboratory validation results. By bridging the gap between theoretical fault analysis and deployment-ready protection schemes, this work serves as a foundational step toward the DC data centers that enable further AI evolution.
